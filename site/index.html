Pre-registered · tested in four markets

Austria's load forecast absorbs snowmaking. Vermont's may not.

Baptiste Cristofari

Independent

August 2026  ·  Energy economics / load forecasting  ·  code and data

In one minute

The question Austrian ski resorts burn about 281 GWh a season making artificial snow, almost all of it on cold November and December nights: 8–15% of overnight demand. The guns run only below a wet-bulb temperature near −2 °C and stop once the base layer is built, so how much power a cold night draws depends on how much snow has already been made.

The answer No. The prediction and the stopping rules were committed before any load data was opened. On thirteen seasons and 780 nights the effect is +5.1 MW, give or take 11.9, and two of three rules fired. The same model on the same nights finds the Christmas industrial shutdown at −274 MW, so it can see effects of the size snowmaking would have to produce. The load is absorbed by the forecast rather than missed by it.

Elsewhere, and in the market Italy-North agrees at +4.3 ± 10.2. Switzerland could never have found the effect. A pre-registered price test cannot tell either: overnight the supply stack is so flat that Austria's entire fleet is worth about €14/MWh against a spread whose swings are three times that.

Section 01

The question

Transmission system operators publish a day-ahead load forecast, and both it and the realised load are free. If snowmaking is invisible to the model, that error should be positive on nights when snowmaking runs, and the size of the miss should depend on the snowpack rather than on temperature alone. The interest is whether a large, lumpy, path-dependent industrial load can hide inside a production forecast, a structure that recurs in Spanish irrigation pumping and North American grain drying.

Section 03

Why a forecast might miss it, and why it might not

The naive version is that load forecasts are temperature models and temperature models have no memory. Two mechanisms argue against it, both written down before the data was opened. A memoryless model still absorbs the average response: the temperature coefficient is fitted on history in which cold nights are snowmaking nights, so the residual holds the snowmaking anomaly rather than the load. Production forecasts are autoregressive: APG publishes at 08:00 on historical actual load, day type and temperature, so a lagged-load term carries a running campaign into tomorrow. Both push α, the share left unexplained, down, and α binds the whole design.

Section 04

Identification

The obvious test, comparing nights just below the wet-bulb threshold to nights just above, is contaminated. At 1,800 m, −2 °C wet bulb is roughly −1 to 0 °C air temperature, and every nonlinearity in heating load lives near freezing: heat-pump COP collapse, defrost cycles, trace heating. Humidity, which enters wet bulb, is itself a load driver.

The identifying variation is therefore not the threshold crossing but its interaction with accumulated season-to-date cold:

err ~ below × cum_cold_hours
      + dist + below:dist + holiday
      + doy + doy² + season FE + dow FE

err            = actual load − day-ahead forecast, MW
below          = 1 if alpine wet-bulb index < −2 °C
dist           = wb_index − (−2)
cum_cold_hours = hours below threshold since 1 Oct, current season

Heating load does not care how many cold hours the season has already delivered. Snowmaking does, because the base gets built and the guns stop. The interaction is the only coefficient in this design a temperature confound cannot produce.

4.1 Building the wet-bulb index

Three choices decide the answer before the econometrics do. Stations are selected by altitude band (900–2,600 m), because valley stations cross the threshold hundreds of hours later than the places snow is made. Wet bulb is corrected for station pressure, since ISA pressure at 1,800 m is 815 hPa and assuming sea level shifts it by 0.2–0.4 °C. And the psychrometric equation is solved rather than approximated, because the Stull (2011) closed form errs by 0.7–1.0 °C below freezing.

Section 05

Pre-registration

Primary prediction. The below × cum_cold_hours coefficient is negative and significant at 5%.

Negative, not positive, and the direction is the whole test. Snowmaking adds load, so a cold night on its own should push the forecast error up. What identifies snowmaking rather than heating is that the push fades: by late December the base is built and the guns are off, so the same weather draws far less power than in November. A fade is a negative number, and a positive interaction is the wrong shape rather than a bigger effect.

Kill criteria, committed before looking:

  • The interaction is zero or positive with a tight confidence interval → no memory effect. Stop. · fired
  • The event-study profile is flat across campaign days → the forecast already absorbs it. Stop. · fired
  • A cold, flat placebo country shows the same jump → the result is heating load. Stop. · not run, moot once the first two fired

All outcomes were committed to publication in advance. A null was named as the modal outcome.

Section 06

Statistical power

Measured on the data, the Austrian forecast is noisier than the German-Luxembourg benchmark of 3.14% used to calibrate this: 6.48% MAE on November–December night hours, 554 MW after fixed effects, raising the pass mark to α ≥ 27.4%. Section 8.1 redoes it on fitted standard errors, which puts the real Austrian bar at 47%.

7.1 Why this is a null rather than an absence of evidence

The specification is not underpowered for effects of the relevant size. On the same 780 nights it recovers the Christmas shutdown at −274 MW with t = −3.3, and the snowmaking interaction is +5 ± 12. Two limits: the Christmas argument works because APG leaves the shutdown in its residual, which Terna and Swissgrid do not, and it is a claim about 274 MW. The smallest seasonal swing the fitted model could have detected at 80% power is 426 MW against a fleet drawing an estimated 900 MW, so Austria could only ever have caught a forecaster missing 47% or more.

How to read the power columns. The coefficient is per 100 accumulated cold hours and a season delivers about a thousand, so the seasonal swing is what has to be detectable, and "needs α" is that divided by each market's coincident snowmaking draw. No test here could see a forecaster missing less than about a quarter of the snowmaking load, and Austria, on the best-grounded fleet estimate of the four, could not see one missing less than half. That is the finding behind all the individual findings.

Italy-North replicates the null at +4.3 (10.2) against Austria's +5.1 (11.9). The Christmas sanity gate is an Austrian regularity, working only because APG's night MAE is 6.48%; Terna's is 2.17% and its forecast predicts −4,461 MW of a −4,482 MW shutdown, so the Italian null carries only a paper-power bound. Switzerland could never have detected the effect at a required α of 201%, and its significant, correctly signed coefficient implies a swing larger than the entire Swiss fleet, so it is a confound.

Vermont meets the prediction, then fails to hold it. ISO-NE's regional forecast is not a fixed load-share allocation, so the share is what the test targets. On five seasons the interaction is −0.0211 percentage points of share per 100 cold hours (HC1 0.0073, p = 0.004), with Rhode Island, a same-forecaster zero-snowmaking placebo in the same feed, null at +0.0019 (0.0037). But Mount Washington in place of the road stations gives −0.0058 (0.0068), the effect sits on its noise floor at −25 MW against 24 MW detectable, and ISO-NE's own zonal report drops it to −0.0281 (0.0188) while firing the placebo. Vermont is suggestive rather than confirmation.

8.2 The price test: overnight, those megawatts are worth almost nothing

A load forecast error is the grid operator's error, not the market's, so a null on load does not rule out a price effect. That test is pre-registered and run in src/price/. It is on the day-ahead spot auction, the only instrument that clears on a snowmaking decision's eighteen-hour horizon. A quarterly baseload futures contract averages away the day-to-day wet-bulb variation the design runs on, so no futures contract is tested. The outcome is the night-minus-midday price spread, which differences out the fuel cost that dominates the level through the crisis winters; the right-hand side is the load test's, unchanged.

All four come back underpowered. The overnight merit order is nearly flat, so Austria's own night data prices a 900 MW fleet at about €14/MWh at the margin against a night-day spread with a €43/MWh standard deviation. A load test can find a few hundred megawatts because load is measured in megawatts; a price test has to find what they are worth. Austria is the near miss at 1.3×. The Christmas gate fails here for a second, independent reason: Christmas removes midday and night demand at once, so an outcome built to ignore common demand shocks ignores the reference effect too.

In Vermont a placebo settled it. Vermont's price interaction is −1.47 (0.54), p = 0.007, the predicted sign, and Rhode Island, which makes no snow, returns −1.33 (0.52), p = 0.011. That is a New England–wide relationship between accumulated cold and the spread, not a Vermont signal. It bites where the load placebo could not, because the eight regional shares sum to zero by construction and force a genuine Vermont effect to push Rhode Island the other way.

8.3 A different treatment: snowfall-forecast revisions

Every test above runs on accumulated cold hours, a slow seasonal variable badly collinear with day-of-season, and that collinearity is what produced required α between 27% and 201%. A fourth arm replaces it with NWS snowfall-forecast revisions: high-frequency news arriving six to ten times a day rather than nine times a season, and a revision to expected natural snowfall holding temperature fixed should not move heating demand at all. Because ISO-NE's day-ahead market closes mid-morning, a revision arriving after the close is information the price cannot contain, which makes a real falsification test available.

On 600 nights across ten Vermont seasons that falsification passes: news postdating the auction moves nothing, −0.86 (2.58). The pre-gate coefficient is +0.20 (2.20), wrong-signed and underpowered by six to eleven times, so it is reported and not read. What the arm adds is a treatment with real high-frequency variation and a falsification test that passes. Full write-up in src/revision/README.md.

Section 09

Limitations

  • The published forecast is the operator's transparency artefact, not the trading consensus. A bias in it shows a blind spot in APG's forecast, not a market mispricing.
  • The treatment is a switch and the physics is a staircase. Olefs et al. (2010), the source of the −2 °C threshold, defines a relationship between wet-bulb temperature and snowmaking capacity alongside it. A binary treatment discards the intensity margin, and much of the megawattage lives there.
  • Water temperature mimics the predicted effect. Production at the margin needs water below about +2 °C and reservoir water cools through the season, so an identical wet bulb is more productive in late December. That needs no path dependence, and nothing here rules it out.
  • No working sensitivity check exists outside Austria, so every replication after the first carries a paper power calculation and no proof its instrument can see anything. cum_cold_hours also ignores melt and water remaining, operators skip marginal nights just below threshold, APG's load excludes a Vorarlberg corridor, the opening-date test was not identifiable from calendar dates (R² = 0.798), and the NL/DK placebo was not run.
Section 10

Data and reproduction

No API token is required. Austrian Power Grid publishes both load series back to 2009 as nested per-year ZIP archives with no registration, and GeoSphere Austria's station API needs no key. One script reproduces everything:

git clone https://github.com/baptistecristo/Electricity-demand-forecasting-in-Austria
pip install -r requirements.txt
python src/apg_pipeline.py

For cross-country work, energy-charts.info serves day-ahead forecast, load and spot price for most European bidding zones with no token. Futures settlement history is paywalled at EEX and ICE, so day-ahead spot is the honest substitute.
